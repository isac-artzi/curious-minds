# Prompt Cards

**Twenty-four starter explorations — six per lab.** Print, cut along the lines,
put a stack on each table. Each card names a grade band, but treat that as a
starting point rather than a ceiling.

> **Turn on 🎯 Challenge mode first.** Every lab has it. It hides the result
> until the student commits to a prediction. Predict-then-check is worth more
> than any other single thing in this app — the surprise is the learning.

---

# 🧪 Chemistry What-If Lab

### C1 · The one everyone knows — *Grades 5–8*
Select **hydrogen** and **oxygen**. Temperature **500 K**. Catalyst: `spark`.
**Predict first:** what forms, and does it release or absorb energy?
> **Then ask:** the equation shows 2 H₂ + O₂ → 2 H₂O. Why *two* hydrogens?

### C2 · Same elements, different story — *Grades 6–9*
Keep hydrogen + oxygen. Now drag temperature to **10,000 K**.
**Predict first:** does water still form?
> **The point:** at plasma temperatures molecules can't hold together. Products
> depend on *conditions*, not just ingredients. This is the card that breaks the
> "chemicals have fixed outcomes" misconception.

### C3 · Why sodium is kept under oil — *Grades 7–10*
**Sodium** + **chlorine**, 298 K, no catalyst.
**Predict first:** two dangerous things make... what?
> **Then ask:** why is table salt safe when both parents are lethal? Answer:
> ionic bonding changes the electron structure entirely. New substance, new
> properties.

### C4 · Watch it label its own uncertainty — *Grades 9–12*
Switch **Mode** to `speculative`. Try **uranium** + **helium** at **10,000 K**.
**Predict first:** will Claude claim to know what happens?
> **The real lesson isn't the chemistry — it's the confidence badges.** Find a
> ⚪ *speculative* claim and ask students how they'd verify it. This is AI
> literacy taught through chemistry.

### C5 · Pressure is a variable too — *AP Chemistry*
**Nitrogen** + **hydrogen**, 700 K, catalyst `iron`. Run it at **1 atm**, then
at **200 atm**.
> **This is the Haber–Bosch process** — the reaction that feeds roughly half of
> humanity. Connect Le Châtelier's principle to the pressure slider, then to
> the global nitrogen cycle.

### C6 · Bring your own unit — *Any grade*
Whatever you taught last week. Build it, run it, then click **💾 Save
experiment** and keep the file.
> **Take-home:** hand that `.curious` file to students. It stores your *setup*,
> not the answer — so they open it and discover the result themselves.

# 🌿 Ecosystem / Biome Lab

### E1 · The famous one — *Grades 5–9*
Biome **Yellowstone**. Include wolves and elk. Disturbance: **apex predator
removal**. Horizon: 25 years.
**Predict first:** what happens to the elk? To the *trees*?
> **Then ask:** how does removing a predator change a **river**? (It does. The
> cascade runs wolves → elk → willow → beaver → stream channel.)

### E2 · Invasion — *Grades 6–10*
Biome **freshwater lake** or **mangrove**. Disturbance: **invasive arrival**.
**Predict first:** which native species is most at risk, and why *that* one?
> **Then ask:** what makes an invader successful — something about the invader,
> or something about the ecosystem receiving it?

### E3 · Two degrees — *Grades 7–12*
Any biome. Set climate **ΔT = +2 °C**, leave everything else alone. Horizon 25 years.
**Predict first:** which trophic level feels it first?
> **Then ask:** run it at +2 °C, then +4 °C. Is the damage twice as bad, or
> more than twice? Non-linearity is the whole climate conversation.

### E4 · The one nobody watches — *Grades 8–12*
Build any food web. Now set **decomposer** populations to near zero.
**Predict first:** does anything happen? Decomposers eat nothing that other
species need.
> **Why it matters:** without decomposition, nutrients lock up in dead biomass
> and producers starve. The least charismatic tier is load-bearing.

### E5 · Your own backyard — *Any grade*
Pick the biome closest to where your students live. Populate it with species
they could actually see. Apply **drought** or **wildfire**.
> **Why this lands:** an abstract cascade becomes *their* creek. If a species
> you need is missing, add it — see MODIFYING.md, it's a five-line JSON edit.

### E6 · Argue with it — *Grades 9–12, AP Environmental*
Run any scenario. Find a step in the cascade tagged 🟡 *probable* or ⚪
*speculative*. Ask students to defend or refute it with outside evidence.
> **The card that teaches the most.** Students stop consuming AI output and
> start auditing it. Have them write one paragraph: "This step is probably right
> because…" or "I don't buy this because…"

# 🪐 Planet / Exoplanet Builder

### P1 · Build a second Earth — *Grades 5–8*
Star **Sun**, distance **1.0 AU**, mass **1.0** Earth, atmosphere
**nitrogen–oxygen**, water **70%**.
**Predict first:** habitable? Then move to **0.3 AU** and **2.0 AU**.
> **The Goldilocks zone stops being a phrase** and becomes a range you can find
> by dragging a slider.

### P2 · Around a red dwarf — *Grades 6–10*
Star **Proxima Centauri**. Distance **0.05 AU**.
**Predict first:** that's far closer than Mercury. Is it too hot?
> **The twist:** M-dwarfs are dim, so the habitable zone is *very* close in —
> close enough that planets tidally lock. One face in eternal day, one in
> eternal night. Ask: could life live on the terminator line?

### P3 · Venus, and the runaway — *Grades 7–12*
Earth mass and radius, **0.72 AU**, atmosphere **CO₂-dominant**, water **0%**.
**Predict first:** how different from Earth can it really be?
> **Then ask:** Venus and Earth are nearly twins in size. What made them
> diverge? This is the strongest climate-feedback lesson in the whole app.

### P4 · Spin it — *Grades 8–12*
Build an Earth-like planet, then set rotation period to **2 hours**, then
**8,000 hours**.
**Predict first:** does day length affect *habitability*, or just convenience?
> **It affects everything** — wind, weather, magnetic field generation,
> temperature extremes between hemispheres.

### P5 · Check it against reality — *Grades 9–12*
Build whatever you like, then compare it to a real planet from the NASA
Exoplanet Archive built into the app (try **Proxima Centauri b** or
**TRAPPIST-1e**).
> **The move:** your invented planet is a hypothesis. A real catalogued one is
> evidence. Ask what observation would tell the two apart.

### P6 · Terraform something — *Grades 9–12, Astronomy*
Use the **tweak / terraforming** controls. Take a hostile planet and try to fix it.
**Predict first:** which single change buys you the most habitability?
> **Then ask:** how long would this take, and who pays? Great bridge from
> planetary science into ethics and engineering.

# 🔬 Physics Lab

### F1 · Galileo was right — *Grades 5–8*
Load **🗼 Galileo's Tower**. Drop from 55 m.
**Predict first:** does a heavier ball land sooner?
> **Then:** change `g` to **1.62** (Moon). Same drop, four hundred years of
> physics, one slider.

### F2 · Find the best angle — *Grades 6–10*
Load **🎯 Max-range cannon**. Hold speed at 40 m/s and sweep the angle: 30°,
45°, 60°, 75°.
**Predict first:** which angle throws farthest?
> **Then ask:** why do 30° and 60° land in the same place? (sin 2θ is symmetric
> about 45°.) Students find this themselves — don't tell them.

### F3 · Why the coaster stalls — *Grades 7–10*
Load **🎢 Reaches every hill**, then **🛑 Hill too tall**.
**Predict first:** what has to be true about hill two for the cart to clear it?
> **The insight:** you cannot climb higher than you started. Energy conservation
> becomes visible and obvious rather than an equation to memorize.

### F4 · Why cars crumple — *Grades 8–12*
Compare **🪀 Newton's Cradle** with **💥 Sticky crash**.
**Predict first:** in which is momentum conserved? In which is *energy*?
> **The answer** — momentum in both, kinetic energy only in the elastic one —
> is why crumple zones save lives. Deliberately destroying energy is the design goal.

### F5 · Light doesn't behave — *Grades 10–12, AP Physics*
Load **💡 Just below threshold**, then **💜 UV pops electrons**.
**Predict first:** brighter red light should eventually free an electron, right?
> **It never does.** No intensity of below-threshold light works, while the
> faintest UV does instantly. This killed classical wave theory and won Einstein
> the Nobel — not relativity.

### F6 · The baseball has a wavelength — *Grades 11–12, AP Physics*
Compare **🌊 Electron diffraction** with **⚾ Baseball matter wave**.
**Predict first:** does a thrown baseball have a de Broglie wavelength?
> **Yes — about 10⁻³⁴ m,** which is why you never notice. Quantum mechanics
> doesn't stop applying at human scale; it just stops *mattering*. Best
> scale-intuition card in the deck.

## Using these in class

**The pattern that works:** predict out loud → run it → explain the gap between
prediction and result. The gap is where learning happens; the app just makes the
gap fast and vivid.

**Three moves that raise the ceiling:**
- **Make them find a `speculative` label** and defend or attack it. This is AI
  literacy, taught inside science content.
- **Have them break it.** Ask for a combination the app handles badly. Students
  who find the edges understand the tool — and every model's limits — far better
  than students who only follow the happy path.
- **Save and swap.** Students save a `.curious` file, trade with a partner, and
  predict *each other's* setups before running them.
