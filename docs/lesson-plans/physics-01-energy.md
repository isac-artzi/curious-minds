# You Can't Climb Higher Than You Started

**Grades 7–10 · 50 minutes · 🔬 Physics Lab**

**Standards:** MS-PS3-1, MS-PS3-5, HS-PS3-1, HS-PS3-2 · SEP 2, 4 · CCC Energy and Matter

## Objective
Students will apply conservation of energy to predict whether a rollercoaster
cart clears a given hill, and explain energy loss to friction.

## Why a coaster
Conservation of energy is usually taught as an equation to rearrange. Here it's a
yes-or-no question with a visible answer: does the cart make it, or does it stall?

## Materials
- One device per pair
- Whiteboards or scratch paper for predictions

## Procedure

**Physical intuition first (5 min).** No devices. "You're on a bike at the top of
a hill. You coast down, then up the next hill. Can the next hill be taller?"

Let them argue. Most will say no, but few can say *why* precisely. That precision
is today's target.

**Establish the rule (10 min).** Load **🎢 Reaches every hill**. Watch the energy
bars trade: potential → kinetic → potential. Name the pattern: total stays
(roughly) constant.

Then load **🛑 Hill too tall**. The cart stalls. Ask what's different — the answer
must be *in terms of height*, not "it didn't have enough speed."

**Prediction rounds (15 min).** Turn on **🎯 Challenge mode**. For each of five
configurations, pairs write CLEARS or STALLS *before* running. Track a running
class score.

Once someone articulates "hill two has to be lower than hill one," the accuracy
jumps visibly — and that's the moment the rule became theirs.

**Complicate it (10 min).** Introduce friction. Now a hill *slightly* lower than
the start can still stall the cart.

Ask the key question: **where did the energy go?** It didn't vanish. Heat, sound,
deformation. Conservation still holds — the accounting just got bigger.

**Formalize (10 min).** Now write it: mgh₁ = ½mv² + mgh₂ + E_lost. Connect each
term to something they watched happen. The algebra lands differently when it
describes a thing they've already seen twenty times.

## Assessment
**Exit ticket:** *"A cart starts at rest at 10 m. Frictionless. Can it clear a
12 m hill? An 8 m hill? Explain both."*

Full credit requires the height comparison **and** the reason. Then: *"Now add
friction. Does your answer to the 8 m hill change?"*

## Differentiation
- **Support:** stick to frictionless. The height rule alone is a full lesson.
- **Extension:** find the exact maximum hill height that still clears with a given
  friction coefficient. Introduces energy budgeting as an optimization problem.

## Connection
Real coasters never have a second hill as tall as the first — chain lift hill is
always the highest point on the track. Pull up any coaster's profile online and
have students verify it. Physics constraints are visible in engineering choices.
