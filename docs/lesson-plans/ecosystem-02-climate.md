# Two Degrees

**Grades 8–12 · 50 minutes · 🌿 Ecosystem / Biome Lab**

**Standards:** MS-ESS3-5, HS-LS2-2, HS-LS2-6, HS-LS2-7 · SEP 4, 6 · CCC Stability and Change

## Objective
Students will analyze how a warming climate propagates through an ecosystem, and
evaluate whether ecological damage scales linearly with temperature.

## The idea worth landing
"Two degrees" sounds small. Students will assume four degrees is exactly twice as
bad. It generally isn't — and discovering that non-linearity themselves is worth
more than being told.

## Materials
- One device per student or pair
- Data table: ΔT column, plus columns for each trophic level
- Graph paper or a spreadsheet

## Procedure

**Anchor (5 min).** "Your body runs at 37 °C. At 39 °C you're sick. At 41 °C
you're in danger. Four degrees is the difference between fine and fatal." Ask
what that suggests about a 4 °C planet.

**Model the method (10 min).** Pick one biome as a class. Run it at **ΔT = 0**
and record which species are stressed. Then **ΔT = +2 °C**. Compare carefully —
which trophic level moved *first*?

Usually producers or temperature-sensitive specialists. Establish that effects
enter the system somewhere specific and travel from there.

**Systematic data collection (15 min).** Each student runs their chosen biome at
**ΔT = 0, +1, +2, +3, +4 °C**, holding everything else constant, recording
outcomes at each step.

Insist on one variable at a time. This is the experimental-design lesson hiding
inside the climate lesson.

**Graph and interpret (10 min).** Plot damage against ΔT. Ask:
- Is your line straight?
- Is there a point where things change abruptly?
- Did any species vanish entirely — and does that change everything downstream?

**Solutions (10 min).** In pairs: given your biome at +3 °C, propose one
intervention. Test it in the app if it's expressible; argue for it if not. Report
back with the tradeoff — every intervention has one.

## Assessment
**Graph + paragraph.** Graph: correctly plotted, axes labeled (3). Paragraph:
describes the shape of the relationship (3), identifies a threshold or tipping
point if present (2), proposes a defensible intervention with a tradeoff (2).

## Handling the politics
Keep the frame on **mechanism**, not policy. "What happens to this food web when
it gets warmer" is a biology question, and the app answers it with confidence
tiers attached. Students who disagree about policy can still agree about trophic
mechanics — and that's a more productive place to start than the reverse.

## Extension
Compare a **tundra** run with a **coral reef** run at identical ΔT. Why is the
same temperature change more destructive in one? Leads directly to thermal
tolerance ranges and specialist-vs-generalist strategy.
