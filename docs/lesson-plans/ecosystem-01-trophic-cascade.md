# The Wolf and the River

**Grades 5–9 · 50 minutes · 🌿 Ecosystem / Biome Lab**

**Standards:** MS-LS2-2, MS-LS2-3, MS-LS2-4 · SEP 2, 7 · CCC Systems, Cause and Effect

## Objective
Students will trace a trophic cascade through at least four steps and argue from
evidence that removing one species changes an entire ecosystem — including its
physical landscape.

## The hook that never fails
Wolves changed the shape of Yellowstone's rivers. It sounds absurd. It's true,
and students can reason their way to it.

## Materials
- One device per pair
- Blank cascade map: five boxes connected by arrows
- Optional: the classic "How Wolves Change Rivers" video as a closer

## Procedure

**Predict cold (5 min).** Before any devices: "Yellowstone loses every wolf.
Name three things that change." Collect answers. Almost all will be about elk or
deer. Write them down — you'll compare later.

**Establish the baseline (10 min).** Project the lab. Biome **Yellowstone**,
wolves and elk present, no disturbance, 25-year horizon. Run it. This is a
healthy system — establish what "stable" looks like before breaking it.

**Break it (10 min).** Turn on **🎯 Challenge mode**. Apply disturbance **apex
predator removal**. Before revealing, have each pair write a prediction for
*elk*, *willow/aspen*, and *the river*.

Reveal. Walk the cascade one step at a time — do not skip ahead:
> wolves ↓ → elk ↑ → willow and aspen browsed down → beaver lose building
> material → dams disappear → stream channels widen and erode

**The moment:** ask who predicted the river. Usually nobody. That gap is the
lesson: **systems connect in ways that aren't obvious from the parts.**

**Pairs investigate (15 min).** Each pair picks a different biome and removes
*its* apex predator — kelp forest (sea otter), coral reef (shark), savanna
(lion). They map the cascade on the blank sheet, minimum four steps.

**Share-out (10 min).** Each pair presents their cascade in 60 seconds. Build a
class claim: *what do apex predators do?* Push past "they eat things" toward
regulation, structure, and stability.

## Assessment
**Cascade map** — scored on: four or more linked steps (4) · each arrow labeled
with a mechanism, not just a direction (3) · at least one non-obvious consequence
such as a physical or landscape change (3).

## Differentiation
- **Support:** provide the first two cascade steps; students complete the rest.
- **Extension:** run the *same* biome with a **50% habitat loss** disturbance
  instead. Which does more damage — losing the predator, or losing the space?
  Have them defend an answer.

## Common student misconception
*"The ecosystem will just rebalance."* Have them run a 25-year horizon and look
at whether it does. Some systems recover; some shift to a new stable state and
stay there. That distinction — hysteresis — is a genuinely advanced idea that
students can *see* here.
